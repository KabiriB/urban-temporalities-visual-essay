# Final ACMS Visual Essay — Design Doctrine

## Status
Canonical design branch: `final-acms-edition`
Archival original: `archive/october-2026-original`
Source ancestor: 5 October 2026 publication commit `aa3af62416505a7a0daca0289a826338cc499136`

## North star
**The map is the protagonist.**

This is not a portfolio of visual-storytelling techniques and not a generic premium scrollytelling site. It is an authored visual argument about a single proposition:

> A boundary can tell us where a jurisdiction stops. It cannot tell us where a life stops.

Every interaction, image, quotation, statistic, transition and annotation must make that argument more legible. If removing an element does not weaken the reader's understanding, remove it.

## What the October original understood
1. It had one governing intellectual sequence: city edge -> life geography -> belonging -> institutional plurality -> synthesis.
2. It was structurally economical: 11 major visual sections rather than 17.
3. It gave the Atlas centre-stage and described the visual grammar plainly: begin with the city, change the line, then change the dimension of the life.
4. It retained the five dimensions of life geography — Movement, Household, People, Support, Future — instead of reducing the life geography mainly to future destinations.
5. It allowed prose to explain concepts without continually creating a new visual mode.
6. It ended with a modest claim rather than trying to turn every finding into a spectacle.

## What the November rebuild improved
1. Stronger direct archival quotations and better human texture.
2. A clearer staged boundary sequence.
3. A powerful line: “Nothing about the place changed. The boundary did.”
4. Better articulation of the future-horizon result.
5. More explicit methods, disclosure thresholds and distinction between mapped Future destinations and the 58.4% future-orientation measure.
6. Improved mobile and responsive engineering in several places.

These improvements should be retained **as evidence and editorial material**, but not as 17 separate visual set-pieces.

## What the November rebuild lost
1. **Map primacy.** The map became an illustration beside explanatory prose.
2. **Conceptual economy.** Too many sections, headline pauses, quote panels, cards and visual resets.
3. **The plurality of life geography.** Movement, Household, People and Support receded while Future became disproportionately dominant.
4. **Continuity.** The reader repeatedly leaves one visual grammar and enters another.
5. **Discovery.** Several conclusions are stated before the reader has experienced the evidence.
6. **Restraint.** The build became more visibly “designed” while becoming less intellectually distinctive.

## Evidence authority
The final edition must use the **latest reviewed evidence**, not October values merely because the October design is being restored.

The current production methods are authoritative unless superseded by a later verified audit:
- raw survey file: 1,644 records;
- descriptive geography: 1,643 records with valid study city and exactly one valid study neighbourhood;
- item-specific denominators vary;
- 110 interview and focus-group transcripts;
- quotations anonymous;
- Nairobi excerpts use reviewed English translations;
- city-edge layers remain analytically distinct;
- mapped life geographies are aggregates, not individual trajectories;
- places require at least five unique respondents or are combined/withheld;
- curves indicate relational reach, not literal travel routes;
- the 58.4% measure and the mapped Future layer are different analytical objects;
- nine neighbourhoods were purposively selected, so findings are not city-wide prevalence estimates.

## Final visual grammar

### 1. One spatial stage
The principal visual field is a map that persists while its meaning changes. Avoid creating a new panel whenever the argument changes.

### 2. Controlled revelation
Reveal only one new concept at a time. The reader should first see a change, then receive the shortest annotation needed to understand it.

### 3. Same place, changing definition
Administrative -> built-up morphology -> functional urban area. The geography stays still while the definition changes.

### 4. Same life, changing geography
After the city-edge sequence, keep the same spatial stage and reveal the geography of life. The crucial contrast is not “map versus text” but “territorial edge versus relational reach.”

### 5. Time must alter geography
The 2-year, 10-year and retirement findings should not be three statistic cards. The same future geography should expand through time. Percentages annotate the transformation after the reader sees it.

### 6. Qualitative material enters as evidence, not decoration
Participant voices should interrupt or deepen a mapped moment. Avoid standalone quotation galleries. Use only the strongest verified excerpts.

### 7. Photography is episodic
Photography should mark a genuine change in register — opening, one or two human interruptions, closing — not fill visual gaps.

### 8. Statistics are annotations before they are components
Prefer spatial annotation, direct labels and integrated quantitative marks. Standalone cards and counters require a clear analytical reason.

### 9. Interaction earns its existence
No hover-only discoveries. No animation for atmosphere alone. Every interactive state must answer a research question or reveal a relationship.

### 10. The Atlas is the release of agency
The authored essay leads first. Only after the argument is understood does the reader receive the full Atlas to explore Accra, Johannesburg and Nairobi independently.

## Storyboard v1

### Scene 0 — Prologue
Full-bleed field photograph. Minimal copy.
“Urban Temporalities · 2018–2026”
“The City Does Not Contain the Life”
One short sentence situating the eight-year question.

### Scene 1 — The first edge
Map opens on Johannesburg. Berea, Diepsloot and Katlehong are visible. No competing cards or side graphics.
Question: **Where does the city end?**

### Scene 2 — Jurisdiction
Administrative boundary appears.
Annotation: what this line knows — municipal responsibility, planning, elections, services.

### Scene 3 — Settlement
Without replacing the map, built-up morphology appears.
The administrative line remains as context.
Reader sees that physical urbanisation exceeds legal jurisdiction.

### Scene 4 — Function
Functional urban area appears.
Again, same map.
Only now explain that it is a harmonised model of the wider urban system.

### Scene 5 — Hinge
Strip away explanatory clutter.
“Nothing about the place changed.
**The boundary did.**”

### Scene 6 — Locate the life
Keep the same spatial world.
Anchor current residence.
Introduce the proposition: **A person does not have one geography.**

### Scene 7 — Five dimensions
Reveal Movement, Household, People, Support and Future sequentially.
Do not show everything simultaneously.
Each dimension changes the relational field while the city edge remains visible.

### Scene 8 — Time changes where
Use Future as the first deep dive.
2 years -> 10 years -> retirement.
The geography expands in place.
Only after each reveal annotate the beyond-current-city share.

### Scene 9 — Belonging without containment
Bring in neighbourhood belonging and 58.4% future-elsewhere result.
The visual point: local belonging and elsewhere-oriented futures coexist.

### Scene 10 — Human interruption
One carefully selected archival voice attached to a specific mapped place.
The participant changes the meaning of the map rather than pausing it for decoration.

### Scene 11 — Plural routes
Only if the evidence materially advances the central argument:
show how different problems route people through different relational/formal infrastructures.
Use one compact visual grammar; no new “dashboard”.

### Scene 12 — Three-city reprise
Accra, Johannesburg, Nairobi.
Same visual grammar, not three new interfaces.
The reader sees the argument travel across the study without claiming equivalence or prevalence.

### Scene 13 — Synthesis
“We began by asking what mobility does to urban life.”
Administrative city -> settlement -> urban system -> geography of life.
“Their lives were not placeless.
**They were multiply placed.**”

### Scene 14 — Release the Atlas
The authored narrative ends.
Now give the reader control:
city, edge definition, life dimension, nodes and relational reach.
The Atlas becomes an analytical appendix that can be explored rather than a diversion from the essay.

### Scene 15 — Close
Final field photograph.
“A boundary can tell us where a jurisdiction stops.
**It cannot tell us where a life stops.**”

### Scene 16 — Methods
Compact, rigorous, expandable.
Sampling, denominators, qualitative archive, city-edge sources, aggregation/disclosure rules, scope, acknowledgements.

## Quality gate before full build
Do **not** build the whole essay next.

Build only Scenes 0–6 as a reference specimen:
Prologue -> Johannesburg -> Administrative -> Built-up -> Functional -> Hinge -> Locate the life.

The specimen must pass all of these tests before the rest is built:
1. The map dominates without making the prose illegible.
2. The visual thesis is understandable without reading long paragraphs.
3. Every transition communicates a conceptual change.
4. Desktop and mobile are separately art-directed.
5. Scroll never feels ornamental.
6. The boundary geometries remain accurate and visually distinguishable.
7. No section exists merely because it is attractive.
8. The experience feels calm enough to sustain attention.
9. Reduced-motion mode remains fully intelligible.
10. The first major revelation produces a genuine change in understanding, not merely a visual effect.

## Non-negotiable editorial test
For every element ask:

> If we removed this interaction, movement, photograph, statistic, quotation or map transition, would the reader understand the intellectual argument less well?

If the answer is no, remove it.
