The City Does Not Contain the Life

What eight years of following urban lives across Accra, Johannesburg and Nairobi taught us about the edges of the city.
A visual essay
By Kabiri N. Bule · October 2026
Photograph · Loren Landau

Opening field photograph from the Urban Temporalities project showing women walking through an Accra street.

2018 · The question

In 2018, we began with a question. We wanted to understand what mobility does to urban life: to belonging, relationships, political community and the ways people imagine their futures. Our proposal imagined following people’s multiple sites of engagement within and beyond Accra, Johannesburg and Nairobi, and finding ways to make those connections visible.

We did not know what the answer would look like. The project changed as it unfolded: methods shifted, COVID intervened, and the cities repeatedly resisted the neat conceptual containers we brought to them.

Eight years later, one question has stayed with me:

Can an urban life really be contained by the city in which it is lived?

Increasingly, our answer is: not necessarily.

Where does the city end?

Before locating the life, we had to decide what we meant by “the city”.

One answer is administrative: the legal jurisdiction through which local government operates. Another is physical: buildings, roads and settlements often continue across those legal lines. Then there is the wider urban system of interaction connecting places through work, movement and economic activity.

These are not competing attempts to discover the one true city. They answer different questions.

01 · JURISDICTION

Administrative city

The legal city. It tells us where formal local-government responsibility begins and ends: services, elections, planning and accountability.

02 · MORPHOLOGY

Built-up city

The continuous physical urban landscape. It follows settlement and urban fabric rather than a municipal line.

03 · FUNCTION

Functional city-region

A harmonised model of the wider urban system of interaction. In this essay we use a 2015 modelled functional urban area for cross-city comparison.

Then we located the life

A person does not have one geography.

The people we studied were giving us another map. The survey asked not only where someone came from and lived now, but where movement histories, households, recent connections, support and imagined futures reached.

Change the dimension of the life and the geography changes. Movement, household, people, support and future do not necessarily coincide. Yet all belong to the same life.

Belonging without containment

Distributed life did not mean detachment from where people lived.

Across all three city samples, most respondents said they felt they belonged in their neighbourhood.

58.4%

Among respondents who said they belonged in their neighbourhood and answered at least one question about the future, 58.4% imagined at least one future elsewhere — in a community of origin, elsewhere in the home country, or in another country.

Belonging here did not require imagining the whole life here.
The human geography

The interviews made those combinations legible.

In one Kayole focus group, some participants valued children growing up locally because the neighbourhood taught resilience, even while imagining that they might later move elsewhere. Others wanted their children to leave. The disagreement mattered: attachment and future orientation were not opposites.

Spatial life was distributed.
Institutional life often was too.
More than one route through urban life

Plurality is not the same thing as absence.

Residents also described neighbourhood life as something learned relationally. In Kayole, newcomers had to understand local routines, risks and who could help them navigate the area. Depending on the problem, help might involve relatives, neighbours, landlords, religious organisations, councillors, police, courts or other intermediaries.

Some routes worked. Others failed. Formal institutions could be essential while also being difficult, slow or risky to reach.

The implication is modest but important: a city may look disordered when viewed through an expectation of one territory, one authority and one route into political life. Following people closely can reveal pattern where our categories initially see fragmentation.

What we are not claimingThree cities cannot stand in for a continent. This project does not offer a universal replacement model for African or “Global South” urbanism, and it does not treat all informal or relational arrangements as benign.
At project close

People can be deeply rooted where they live while relationships, obligations, institutional routes and futures remain distributed across other places.

Perhaps the city was never large enough to contain everything we were trying to see.

A boundary can tell us where a jurisdiction stops. It cannot tell us where a life stops.

About the research · how to read the maps

Study frame. This visual essay draws on survey, qualitative and spatial work across nine neighbourhoods in Accra, Johannesburg and Nairobi. Survey percentages use the cleaned analytic sample (n=1,623), with item-specific valid denominators. They describe the study samples rather than the populations of the three cities.

Qualitative material. The qualitative corpus contains 110 transcripts. Interviews and focus groups are used here to illuminate configurations and mechanisms, not to estimate prevalence. The two qualitative moments in this essay are paraphrases from translated transcripts rather than direct participant quotations.

City edges. Administrative jurisdiction, Africapolis 2020 built-up morphology and a 2015 modelled functional urban area are kept separate because they answer different questions. The functional layer is a harmonised model, not observed commuting. For Accra, the administrative boundary is not displayed because the available geometry could not be verified to the same standard as the other city-edge layers; rather than approximate it, we leave that layer unavailable.

Life geography. The map shows aggregates rather than individual trajectories. A place appears only when at least five unique respondents are represented; otherwise locations are combined at country level or withheld. Curves show relational reach, not literal travel routes.

Future orientation. For the 58.4% measure, we counted respondents who reported neighbourhood belonging, answered at least one future question, and located at least one future in their community of origin, elsewhere in the home country, or in another country. The mapped Future layer is a different object: it uses named destinations that passed spatial and disclosure checks. The statistic and the map therefore should not be read as the same measure.

About this edition. This essay presents only spatial layers that meet the project's public-display and disclosure standards. Where a comparable boundary or destination could not be verified to the same standard, it is omitted rather than approximated.
